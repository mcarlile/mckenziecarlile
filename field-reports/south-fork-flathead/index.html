South Fork of the Flathead

Montana · Packrafting · August 4–10, 2022

7 days of packrafting through Montana's Bob Marshall Wilderness.

When six friends need a way to celebrate turning 30 together, the options are plentiful. We'd thrown around backpacking in Glacier and Andrew Skurka's High Route through the Wind Rivers, but when Ben and Kelsey proposed a 7-day packrafting trip through the Bob Marshall Wilderness on the South Fork of the Flathead, it was an easy yes.

Ben had introduced the group to packrafting on a Colorado River trip a few years back. He and Kelsey had been exploring Montana rivers while living in Gardiner until the historic 2022 flooding upended their plans. The South Fork felt like a natural culmination — deeper into the wilderness than any of us had gone by boat.

Getting There

Logistics came together over months of group chat threads. Josh provided an RV base in Kalispell. Matt from Backcountry Packrafts shipped boats to Rocky Mountain Outfitters. We arranged invasive species inspections and lined up a local shuttle service so we wouldn't spend two days driving vehicles around. I arrived at 1:30 AM after a late-night Google Maps confusion, then woke at 4:30 for our departure.

Day 1 — August 4

We drove Montana Highway 83 in the dark, past Swan Lake and Condon, stopping for breakfast at Pop's Place in Seely Lake. The Lodgepole Creek trailhead at the end of forest road 4388 is where the trip really started — Ben and Kelsey waiting with boats already inflated.

The hike in was a suffer-fest. Packs were heavy, the burn area offered zero shade, and the miles felt longer than the map suggested. We forded Young's Creek at a wide, shallow crossing and made camp on the far bank. Finding trees solid enough for bear hangs in the fire-killed forest was its own comedy — we built progressively more ridiculous structures out of deadfall before something finally worked.

Paella and negronis for dinner. The sun went down, the moon came up, and everyone was asleep before it got dark.

Day 3 — August 6

At the confluence of Young's and Danaher creeks, Josh and I caught our first westslope cutthroat — native fish in clear water, exactly what you come to the Bob for. Progress on the river was smoother than the first days, less boot-scooting over shallows.

A rainstorm moved in around river mile 92. We pulled off and waited it out under trees, then paddled on to the Big Meadow work camp ranger station. Ranger Sam was warm and unhurried — showed us around the facility, let us watch the mule pack train come in, then another storm rolled through and he invited us inside to wait it out. These are the encounters that don't make it into the brochure.

We camped at river mile 90. I caught a couple more cutthroat for dinner to stretch the pad thai, and Ben and Kelsey made foraged berry cocktails. We were settling into a rhythm.

Day 4 — August 7

Ice on the boats and tents. The kind of morning where no one moves until Ben gets a fire going for coffee. When the sun finally hit the river the fog burned off into something beautiful.

The paddling was excellent — long deep pools, fast clean lines. The rocks along this section of river have a distinctive multicolored look, almost like fruity pebbles underfoot in the shallows. Near the White River confluence the current picked up significantly. White River drains from the west slope of the Chinese Wall; during high flow it runs milky white from the limestone bottom. We swam and walked boats around a couple wrong forks as the volume increased.

We overshot our target camp near Salmon River Cabin (river mile 77) and landed above the Big Salmon Creek confluence instead. A pack train passed through camp in the evening. Jambalaya for dinner.

Day 5 — August 8

Zero day. We slept in, relocated to a lower camp with river access, and found a swimming hole with a boulder big enough to jump from. Alex, Andrew, and I spent part of the afternoon building a stone oven on the bank — improvised from river rock, designed to bake fish.

In the afternoon we hiked to Big Salmon Lake for a communal 30th birthday ceremony, stopping to eat thimbleberries along the trail with Mount Charlotte behind us. On the way back I broke my tenkara rod and net going after a rising fish, borrowed Josh's gear, and after 45 minutes of careful stalking finally landed a cutthroat.

We baked the fish in the stone oven with olive oil and Crazy Jane's salt. It was the most delicious trout I've ever eaten camping. Then Alex produced a pineapple upside-down cake and secret cigars from his pack as his "x-factor" contribution. Kelsey patched up a toe I'd sliced on a rock. It was a very good day.

Day 6 — August 9

Miles 76 to 69 were the best paddling of the trip — alternating deep pools and fast water. At the Snow and Helen creeks confluence there's a gorge where the walls come straight down to the water. We swam in the cold pool at the base and lounged on the rocks.

Found a perfect campsite between miles 68 and 67 with an unobstructed view of the river. We upended the packrafts and lay on the hulls in the afternoon sun. I introduced friendship bracelet kits as my "x-factor" — more interest than completed bracelets, but the gesture landed. Agave-berry-vodka cocktails before dinner, mac and cheese after, fire until I fell asleep in my chair.

Day 7 — August 10

Last day on the water. We woke knowing it and paddled slowly. Rain threatened but never quite arrived. The section around river mile 63 had some spicy water — everyone upright. We pulled out at the Mid Creek takeout at river mile 61.5.

The hike through the burned lodgepole and fireweed back to the trailhead felt like a slog after a week of floating. At the final bridge near the trailhead, we looked down into the Meadow Creek gorge — sheer walls, no portage route, beyond our grade. Something for another trip.

Both shuttle vehicles materialized as we pulled off our packs. Car beers emerged. Rain intensified on the 3-hour dirt road drive to Kalispell, where we finished the trip at Bonsai Brewing with dinner and more beers.

A college co-worker once told me that turning 30 was like reaching Plymouth Rock after years at sea — not a loss but an arrival, the beginning of something rather than the end. Spending a week in the Bob Marshall Wilderness with people I love feels like the right way to start the decade. We keep finding new skills that get us further into the wilderness. There's a lot left to see.
